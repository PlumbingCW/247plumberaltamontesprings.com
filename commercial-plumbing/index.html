Your business is flooding or backing up right now

People first, property second. These steps apply whether you own the building or lease a suite in it.

1

Clear the area

Move customers and staff away from standing water, especially near floor drains, kitchen equipment and outlets. Wet tile is a slip hazard, and water near electrical gear is a reason to call 911.

2

Stop using the drains

Do not flush, run the dish machine or dump mop water down a floor sink. Every added gallon pushes more wastewater toward the opening that is already overflowing.

3

Close the water

Shut the building's main valve, or the fixture's own stop valve if that is the source. In a multi-tenant building, find out whether your suite has its own shutoff or the landlord controls it.

4

Capture the evidence

Photograph the water line, the fixture and any wet stock or equipment before cleanup. Note the time the problem began. Insurers and landlords will ask for both.

Finding leaks inside commercial walls and ceilings

Commercial buildings hide pipe in ceiling plenums, chases and slab trenches, so a leak may show up two rooms from its source. Stained ceiling tiles, a musty smell and a water meter that spins with every fixture closed all point to a supply or drain leak.

Acoustic listening, thermal imaging and pressure testing narrow a leak to a short section before anyone opens a ceiling. That is the job of leak detection, and the same methods work in retail and office spaces.

Drains, floor sinks and kitchen lines

A floor sink or hub drain handles condensate, mop water and equipment drains, and each one carries more fat and food than a house drain. Slow drains, gurgling and sewer smell in a kitchen usually mean a line coated with grease or a trap that has overfilled.

A cable clears a blockage. High-pressure water scours the pipe wall, which is why hydro-jetting is the usual follow-up for lines that keep coming back. A camera shows whether a broken joint or root is the real cause.

Grease trap and interceptor cleaning for restaurants

A grease trap catches fat, oil and food solids before they reach the sewer. Small units sit under a prep sink, and larger interceptors sit below grade near the kitchen. When the trap is full, grease passes straight through and builds up in the drain line.

Cleaning means pumping out the contents, scraping the walls and baffles, and checking the inlet and outlet tees. Your cleaning schedule is set by your local rules, your hauler agreement and how heavy your cooking load is. Ask the city or your landlord which rule applies to your building before you pick a schedule.

Backflow testing and your water utility

A backflow preventer stops used or contaminated water from flowing backward into the public supply. Florida Administrative Code 62-555.360 requires community water systems to run a cross-connection control program. It also says non-residential backflow preventers are tested after installation or repair and at least annually, while residential devices are tested at least every two years.

The utility for a business here is the City of Altamonte Springs itself. Its Water Distribution office, (407) 571-8340, is the contact for device records. Ask what paperwork they want for your device, then call us to look at its location and condition, since a corroded or leaking assembly will fail a test.

Commercial work is held to the same plumbing code as homes, the International Plumbing Code (IPC) as adopted in Florida, and grease interceptor and backflow rules are checked by the local authority on top of it.
