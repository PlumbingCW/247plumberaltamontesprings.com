Meter still moving with every tap off

The fastest home test takes five minutes. Shut off every faucet, the ice maker, and the irrigation timer, then open the meter box and look at the small leak indicator on the dial. If that triangle or star keeps turning, water is escaping somewhere past the meter.

In Altamonte Springs the city runs the water system, and Water Distribution at (407) 571-8340 maintains the meter itself. Anything on your side of it is yours to fix, and that is where we come in. Take a photo of the dial before and after a one-hour wait so we can see the pace of the loss.

A high bill with nothing wet in sight

A bill that jumps 30 to 40 percent without a change in habits usually means a toilet flapper, a pressure-stressed joint, or a slow line leak. Toilets are the cheapest to rule out. Drop a few drops of food coloring in the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking.

If the toilets are clean, the meter test points to a leak in the house or the yard. Ask the city's utility billing office whether a leak adjustment is available once the repair is done, and keep your repair invoice for that conversation.

Wet spots on ceilings and walls

A brown ring on a ceiling or a bubbled patch of paint is the late stage of a leak that has been feeding for days. The stain often sits several feet from the real source because water runs along joists before it drips. A moisture meter and thermal camera tell us far more than pressing on drywall.

A supply line leak behaves differently from a drain leak. Supply leaks keep running at all hours and show a steady wet pattern. Drain leaks appear only after a shower, a flush, or a dishwasher cycle, which is why we ask when the stain grows. If the survey shows old supply lines failing in several places, a whole-house repipe may cost less than chasing one leak after another.

Leaks under floors and inside slabs

Warm spots on tile, a faint hiss when the house is quiet, and a meter that spins at night all point to a pressurized line below the floor. Hot water lines under concrete are the usual suspects because heat and movement wear the pipe. Our acoustic gear listens to the slab and marks the loudest point within a few inches.

Once the leak is located, the repair choices are spelled out on our slab leak repair page, from a spot fix to rerouting the line overhead. Shutting the main valve until we arrive keeps the loss from growing.

Once the leak is found, the repair follows the plumbing code, Florida's adopted version of the International Plumbing Code (IPC). Detection is the part that carries no code number: it is a diagnosis, and it should be documented.

Buried lines, irrigation, and pools

A soggy patch of lawn on a dry week, a sprinkler zone that sounds weaker than it used to, or green grass in a stripe over a pipe path can all signal a buried leak. Sandy Florida soil drains quickly, so a leaking yard line may show no puddle at all. We pressure-test the line and, where needed, use a tracer gas that rises through the soil to reveal the spot.

Irrigation leaks surge in the wet season, which typically begins in late May, because a broken head hides in the runoff. Check the current watering days with your county or water district before you test zones.

Pool and spa water loss

A pool that drops more than about a quarter inch a day beyond normal evaporation may have a plumbing leak in the return or skimmer line. We pressure-test those lines separately from the shell.

Leaks inside the meter box

Wet soil around the meter box may come from your side or the city side. Call Water Distribution first if water seems to come from the meter itself.

Signs a leak is hiding in your plumbing

Any two of these together justify a call.

  • A water meter leak indicator that keeps turning with all fixtures off.
  • A monthly bill that rises with no change in household use.
  • Warm patches on a floor or a sound of running water in a quiet house.
  • Musty smell, peeling paint, or baseboards that swell and lift.
  • Low water pressure that appeared suddenly at every fixture.
  • Water heater that runs more often or refills when no one is using hot water.
  • Wet or unusually green soil along the line from meter to house.

Active leak right now: what to do while you wait

1

Close the main valve

Turn the shutoff near the meter or where the line enters the house a quarter turn until it stops. Running a faucet afterward drains remaining pressure.

2

Switch off power near wet areas

If water is near outlets, a panel, or appliances, turn off those breakers only if the panel itself is dry.

3

Protect what you can

Move rugs and electronics, set out buckets, and lay towels along baseboards to slow the spread.

4

Photograph the damage

Take dated photos of wet areas and the meter dial. They help with an insurance claim and with locating the source.
