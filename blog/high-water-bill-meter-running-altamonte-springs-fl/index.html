Bills and meters

Water Bill Jumped or Meter Keeps Moving? Find the Leak

If your water meter keeps moving when every fixture is off, water is leaking somewhere on your side of the meter. The fastest way to confirm is a ten-minute test: shut everything off, read the meter, wait, and read it again. A change means a leak. No change means the extra use may be real, or the leak runs only when a timer or float valve opens.

Toilets and irrigation cause most surprise bills, so test those next. The steps below go from simple to complex.

The ten-minute meter test

You need a flashlight, a pen and paper, and a few minutes with no one using water.

  • Turn off all faucets, the dishwasher, the washing machine and the ice maker. Do not flush any toilet.
  • Open the meter box lid. Use a screwdriver to lift it gently, and watch for insects or spiders before you reach in.
  • Find the low-flow indicator on the meter face, a small triangle, star or dial that spins with even tiny flow. If it moves, water is flowing.
  • Write down the numbers on the register, and note whether the meter reads in gallons or cubic feet.
  • Wait fifteen to thirty minutes without using water, then read it again.
  • If the number changed or the indicator moved the whole time, close your home's main shutoff and check again. If the meter stops, the leak is inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is on the line between the meter and the house.

Reading what the test tells you

A slow, steady spin usually means a leaking toilet or a dripping valve. A fast spin points toward a broken line, a stuck irrigation valve or a running water heater relief valve. Intermittent movement suggests an automatic device that opens on its own, such as a sprinkler zone or a toilet that refills in cycles.

If the meter is still moving after you close the main shutoff at the house, the leak is outside the walls. That sits on the buried line, and a water line repair visit finds and fixes it. If the leak turns out to be inside, a pressure test with a gauge narrows down which branch is losing water.

The toilet dye test

A silent toilet leak is the most common reason for a spike in use. The rubber flapper at the bottom of the tank wears out and lets water trickle into the bowl. You will not hear it, and the tank refills in short bursts.

Remove the tank lid, add about ten drops of food coloring to the tank water, and do not flush. Wait fifteen to twenty minutes. If color shows up in the bowl, the flapper or flush valve is leaking. A toilet repair often means a new flapper or fill valve, which is a short job.

Checking your irrigation system

Sprinkler systems leak without anyone noticing. A cracked head, a broken lateral line or a valve that does not close fully can run for hours. Turn off the irrigation controller, then close the irrigation shutoff if there is one, and read the meter again.

If the meter stops, the sprinklers are the leak. Walk each zone while it runs and look for geysers, pooled water or dry patches. The St. Johns River Water Management District sets watering limits across the region, and Seminole County has its own rules, so check the current schedule before you run a test.

Ask the city whether your irrigation runs on potable or reclaimed water. The city says reclaimed water travels through the same distribution system as drinking water, so the answer affects which line you are testing.

What Altamonte Springs customers should know about bills and meters

The City of Altamonte Springs supplies drinking water and sewer service and bills for it. Its Water Distribution Division maintains the pipes, valves and meters that deliver water to your property, and you can reach it at (407) 571-8340. If you suspect the meter itself is faulty, that is the office to call.

The city also posts an online Utility Billing Adjustment Request Form. We could not read the policy terms, so ask the city what documents it wants, such as a plumber's repair invoice, and how a leak adjustment is calculated. Keep your repair receipt and the before-and-after meter readings in one folder.

If the leak turns out to be a failed fitting inside the house, the repair is done to the plumbing volume of the Florida Building Code, itself built on the IPC.

Sewer charges may be tied to water use on your bill. Ask the city how irrigation or a repaired leak is treated before you assume the whole bill will drop.

Finding a leak you cannot see

If the toilets are dry, the irrigation is off and the meter still turns, the leak is hidden. A technician uses acoustic listening gear to hear water escaping from a pipe, a thermal camera to see temperature patterns on floors and walls, and a pressure test to isolate each section of line. That is the work of leak detection.

The result is a marked spot on the floor, wall or yard, not a guess. Bring your meter readings and the last few water bills so the technician can see how long the leak has been running.

When the high bill is not a leak

A bill can also rise from real use: a new pool fill, a houseguest stretch, a run of hot days with more irrigation, or a billing period that was longer than usual. Compare the dates on this bill to the last two.

If the meter test passes and the dye test is clear, ask the city to confirm the meter read. Irrigation use tends to climb in dry weather, so check your timer first. Our crews serving Winter Springs and Lake Mary run these tests every week.
