How Electronic Leak Detection Finds a Hidden Leak Without Demolition

Electronic leak detection uses sensors, microphones, gas, and heat cameras to locate a leak inside a wall, under a slab, or in a buried line, so only a small patch gets opened. Instead of guessing, the technician narrows a leak to within a foot or so before any digging starts.

The tool depends on the pipe and the problem. Below is what each method does, what it cannot do, and typical cost ballparks. If you suspect water is running right now, call (407) 964-8940.

First confirm you actually have a leak

Detection costs money, so rule out the easy explanations first. Turn off every tap and appliance, then watch the meter's small leak indicator or low-flow dial for movement. If it spins with everything off, water is escaping somewhere past the meter.

Compare your last three bills. A jump of 30 percent or more with no change in habits is a classic sign. Other clues include a warm spot on a floor, a musty smell, bubbling paint, or the sound of a trickle at night.

A toilet flapper that leaks silently into the bowl is the most common false alarm. Drop a few drops of food coloring into the tank and wait 15 minutes.

The tools and what each one finds

No single tool finds every leak. A good visit combines two or three.

Acoustic listening and correlators in plain terms

A pressurized pipe with a hole sounds like a faint hiss. A ground mic or floor sensor, like the handheld unit in the photo above, amplifies it. The technician walks a grid and listens for the loudest point.

A correlator makes it more exact. Two sensors clamp onto valves, hose bibs, or the meter at either end of a pipe. The software measures how long the leak sound takes to reach each one and calculates the distance to the leak.

Plastic pipe carries sound poorly, and city traffic, a running HVAC unit, or a pool pump can drown it out. That is why a quiet window helps, and why a technician may ask you to shut off the pool pump or the irrigation timer.

Tracer gas and thermal imaging when listening fails

Tracer gas is a mix of about 5 percent hydrogen in nitrogen. It is non-toxic and not flammable at that mix. The technician drains the suspect line, fills it with gas, and sweeps a sensitive detector across the floor. Gas escapes upward through the leak and shows up as a spike on the reading.

Thermal cameras show a surface as a color map. A hot-water leak under a slab makes a warm bloom. A cold leak in a wall can show as a cool patch from evaporation. A thermal image is a clue, not a final answer, so a good tech confirms with a moisture meter or sound.

Many leak calls end with a moisture map and a marked spot, which makes the repair smaller, faster, and cheaper.

What to do before the technician arrives

A few minutes of prep makes the visit cleaner and the results more accurate.

  • Write down the meter reading, the time, and what was off when you read it.
  • Close every faucet, and note whether toilets or the ice maker are running.
  • Shut off the irrigation timer and the pool fill valve if you have them.
  • Clear furniture from the suspect room so floors can be scanned.
  • Note any warm floor, damp baseboard, stain, or sound and where it is.
  • Gather photos of past leaks or repairs and any plumbing drawings you have.

Detection is diagnosis, so it has no code number, but any repair that follows is built to the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, an adaptation of the IPC.

Pricing depends on the size of the property, the pipe type, and how many methods are used. A typical visit for a single suspected leak commonly runs from about $150 to $600, while slab or buried-line work can run higher. These are typical ballparks that change with the job, not quotes.

What electronic detection cannot do

It cannot see through every material, and it cannot find a leak that is not leaking at that moment. An intermittent drip from a valve or a leaking drain line may need a camera or a dye test instead. A drain leak is also a different problem from a pressure leak, because gravity lines do not hiss.

Shared walls and neighbors add noise in condos and apartments. Many of the city's 97 multifamily communities fit that situation, so speak with your property manager before scheduling work on a common line.

If you smell gas or see water near electrical panels, leave detection for later. Turn off power to the area if it is safe, and call 911 for gas.

After the leak is located

With the spot marked, the options are spot repair, rerouting the line through a wall or attic, or repiping the section. Small repairs are often a copper coupling or a few feet of new pipe. Repeated leaks in one run suggest the pipe is failing, which is when repiping is worth pricing.
