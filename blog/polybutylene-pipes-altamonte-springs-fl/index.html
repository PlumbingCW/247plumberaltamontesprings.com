Polybutylene Pipes: How to Spot Them and What to Do Next

Polybutylene (PB) is a flexible gray, blue or black plastic water pipe that was installed in many homes mainly in the 1970s and 1980s. It is known for leaking at fittings and splitting without warning. If you find it, plan a replacement, because it does not get better with age.

You can check in about ten minutes by looking at the pipe near the water heater, under sinks and at the main shutoff. The sections below show how to identify it, what the risks are, and how to compare repipe options.

Where to look and what you are looking for

Polybutylene is easy to spot once you know the clues. Check these places and write down what you find.

  • Water heater: look at the pipes going into the top. Gray plastic pipe, flexible and without a rigid glued joint, is a strong hint.
  • Under bathroom and kitchen sinks: check the supply lines that run to the shutoff valves. Polybutylene in these spots is often gray, sometimes blue for cold water.
  • Main shutoff area or utility closet: look where the line enters the house. Black or gray pipe at this point may be polybutylene service line.
  • Markings: the pipe is often stamped with letters such as PB2110, along with the maker's name and a size.
  • Fittings: look for metal crimp rings, or plastic fittings made of acetal. Copper or brass fittings with gray flexible pipe are typical.
  • Attic or crawl space: if you can see the pipe route, flexible gray plastic running in long, gentle curves is a clue.

Why polybutylene fails

Polybutylene reacts with oxidants such as chlorine that are normal in municipal water. Over years, the pipe wall flakes and thins from the inside, and the pipe can look fine from the outside. Plastic fittings can also crack under the stress of crimp rings.

The result is a pipe that may leak at a joint or split along its length. Because the damage is internal, the first sign may be a sudden wet ceiling or floor. Not every home with polybutylene has a leak, but there is no way to tell which pipes will fail next.

A short national history

Polybutylene was sold across the United States as a cheap, easy-to-install alternative to copper, and builders used it widely through the 1970s and into the 1980s. As leak complaints piled up, a class action followed. A 1995 newspaper report describes a $950 million settlement fund that covered homes in 21 states, with Shell supplying the resin and Hoechst Celanese making the fittings.

That report is a secondary source and it does not say anything about Florida in particular. The practical point: if you buy or inherit an older house, assume polybutylene is possible and check for it before closing, not after the first leak.

Three materials are common for a whole-house replacement. The right one depends on budget, access and your preferences.

What a repipe looks like inside your house

The crew shuts off the water, cuts small access holes in drywall near fixtures, and pulls the new lines through walls or the attic. Old pipe is cut out or abandoned in place. Each fixture is reconnected and the system is pressure tested before the drywall is patched.

Most single-family repipes take two to five days, and water is off during working hours. Our repiping work includes patching access holes. Painting is usually separate. Plan to move furniture away from walls and clear under-sink cabinets.

Replacement piping must be a material the plumbing code allows, Florida's adopted version of the International Plumbing Code (IPC), so ask for the pipe type in writing before work starts.

Insurance, selling and the cost of waiting

Some insurers ask about the pipe material, charge more for polybutylene or decline to cover a home that has it. Policies differ, so call your agent before you decide. If you plan to sell, many buyers and inspectors ask about it, and a repipe receipt answers the question.

Waiting carries a cost too. A single leak can mean wet drywall, mold and flooring repair that is far more than the repipe saved. If you cannot repipe now, a leak detection visit and a water-shutoff plan can reduce the risk.

If a polybutylene line lets go

A split polybutylene pipe can dump a lot of water in minutes. Act quickly.

1

Shut off the main

Turn the main valve at the house clockwise until it stops. If it will not turn, call the 24/7 line.

2

Cut the power if water is near outlets

Switch off the breaker for wet areas only if you can reach the panel without standing in water.

3

Open the lowest faucet

This drains the lines and reduces pressure on the leak.

4

Document the damage

Take photos and video before you clean, then call your insurance agent.

A technician can repair the split with a transition fitting as a stop-gap, then price a full repipe. If you are in Maitland or Longwood, our Maitland and Longwood service routes both run from Altamonte Springs.
