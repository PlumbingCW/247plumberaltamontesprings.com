Water heater leaking or dead right now? Start here

1

Close the cold supply valve

Find the shutoff on the pipe entering the top of the tank and turn it clockwise until it stops. This ends the flow feeding the leak.

2

Cut the power or the gas

For an electric heater, switch off its double-pole breaker (usually 30 amps) in the panel. For a gas heater, turn the control knob to off. If you smell gas, leave the house, call 911, then call the gas utility number on your bill.

3

Stop the water from spreading

Move boxes and stored items off the floor. Use a wet vac or towels. A tank can release 40 to 50 gallons, so act on the floor early.

4

Take photos and note the label

Photograph the leak spot, the rating label, and the pipes around it. The label shows the model, size and manufacture date. That helps us bring the right parts on the first trip.

Why a tank water heater stops making hot water

Most no-hot-water calls come down to a short list of failed parts. On an electric tank, two 4,500-watt heating elements, each with its own thermostat, do the work. If the upper thermostat or the high-limit reset trips, the whole tank can go cold. If only the lower element dies, you get a few minutes of hot water and then lukewarm.

A gas tank relies on a pilot or igniter, a thermocouple or flame sensor, and a gas control valve. A weak flame sensor shuts the burner down within seconds because the control cannot confirm fire. A blocked flue or a closed combustion air path does the same thing for safety reasons.

Reading the symptoms: what each one usually means

Water on the floor does not always mean a bad tank. A loose fitting, a weeping valve or a dripping supply line can look the same. If the water seems to come from a pipe rather than the heater, a leak detection visit can trace it before anyone opens a wall.

Warning signs you can spot in the garage or closet

Most heaters give some notice before they quit. Check these once a season, and call if you see more than one.

  • Rust-colored streaks or flaking paint near the bottom seam of the tank.
  • Water stains or a soft spot in the drain pan or the floor under it.
  • A discharge pipe from the relief valve that is wet or crusted with minerals.
  • Rumbling or popping while it heats, which points to sediment.
  • Hot water that is now brown or orange for the first minute.
  • A tank label showing a manufacture date more than 10 years back.

Gas, electric and hybrid heaters: what we repair on each

Gas tank heaters

We test the thermocouple's millivolt output, clean the burner and flame sensor, check the draft at the flue, and replace gas control valves when they fail. We also check the venting, because a heater that backdrafts is a safety issue.

Electric tank heaters

With a meter on the terminals, we check each thermostat, each element and the high-limit. A shorted element can trip the breaker again and again.

Hybrid heat pump heaters

These add a compressor, a fan and an air filter on top of the tank. We handle the plumbing side, the condensate drain and the electric backup elements. Sealed refrigerant work may belong to the manufacturer's service channel, and we will say so up front.

Repair or replace: where we draw the line

A good rule is to repair when the tank shell is sound and the failed part is cheap compared with a new unit. Thermostats, elements, thermocouples, relief valves and anode rods all fit that rule. A tank that leaks from the shell cannot be patched, because the steel liner has failed from the inside.

Age matters too. Tanks usually last 8 to 12 years, and a heater past 10 that needs a gas valve or a second repair in a year is often better swapped. When that is the honest answer, we will say so and walk you through replacing the whole unit with the price first.
