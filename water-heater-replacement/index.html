Tank failed and flooding right now: the first ten minutes

1

Stop the incoming water

Turn the cold supply valve on top of the heater clockwise until it seats. If that valve will not turn, shut the main at the house. Do not force a stuck handle, since a broken stem makes things worse.

2

Remove the power source

Switch off the heater's two-pole breaker if it is electric. For gas, turn the control to off. Never run an empty electric tank, because the elements burn out dry.

3

Do not drain it yet

Water inside can be 120 degrees or hotter. Leave it for us unless water is pouring out, in which case attach a hose to the drain valve and run it outside with care.

4

Record what you see

Photos of the leak, the rating plate and the water damage help with insurance and let us bring the right size. Keep any wet items for the adjuster if a claim is likely.

How a tank swap goes from the old unit to hot water

We begin by confirming the fuel, venting and electrical or gas connections, then drain the old tank through a hose. After disconnecting, we carry the old tank out and set the new one on a fresh pan when the space allows. New flexible connectors, a new relief valve and a proper discharge pipe go in at this stage.

Fill comes next. We open a hot faucet inside to purge air, check every joint for weeping, then restore power or light the burner. On a gas heater we also check the flue draft and the gas line for leaks with a manometer or leak solution.

Many swaps take a few hours, and longer if the venting, the gas line or the shutoff valves need work. We tell you about those extras at the start, so the final price does not surprise you.

Sizing the new tank: gallons and recovery rate

A 40-gallon tank often fits one or two people, a 50-gallon tank fits three or four, and an 80-gallon unit suits a large household with a soaking tub. The number on the label to watch is the first-hour rating, which shows how much hot water the unit can deliver in 60 minutes from cold.

Bigger is not always better. An oversized tank wastes standby heat and costs more, while an undersized one runs out during the second shower. We ask how many people live there, how many bathrooms you have, and whether back-to-back showers and laundry happen together.

What gets replaced, and what we ask you to prepare

A proper replacement is more than the tank. These items usually come off or go in during the visit.

  • The tank itself, hauled away after draining.
  • New flexible water connectors on the hot and cold lines.
  • A new temperature and pressure relief valve and its discharge pipe.
  • A shutoff valve on the cold line if the old one is seized.
  • A drain pan under the tank when the space allows.
  • An expansion tank when the system is closed by a backflow device or check valve.
  • A new gas flex line and sediment trap on gas units.
  • Clear access, 3 feet around the heater, and a pet shut away.

Leak at the base: why a split tank is not a repair job

What happens inside the steel

A tank is a steel shell with a glass lining. Over time, the sacrificial anode rod is eaten away, and water reaches bare steel. Rust then perforates the shell, usually near the bottom where sediment sits.

How to confirm it is the tank

Water coming from the underside of the jacket, or rust-stained insulation, means the shell is gone. A drip at the relief valve or at a pipe connection is a different problem. When in doubt, a technician will test before suggesting a new tank, and we will point you back to a repair visit if the fault turns out to be a part.

Why waiting costs more

A slow leak soaks subfloor and drywall, and a sudden failure can release 40 to 50 gallons at once. The tank itself will not get cheaper by waiting.

Old heater, new heater: when we repair, and what we do not do

If a part is cheap and the tank shell is sound, we will tell you to fix it and keep the heater. Past about 10 years, or with rust at the seams, replacement is the better deal. If you plan the work ahead of time, our water heater installation page covers venting, gas and electric prep.

A panel upgrade is an electrician's job, and a new gas main run is a gas fitter's. A gas smell is a 911 call first, and the utility number on your bill second.
