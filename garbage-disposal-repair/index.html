Jammed, humming or leaking right now: what to do first

1

Cut the power

Switch off the wall switch, then turn off the breaker for the sink circuit if you can. Unplug the cord under the sink if it uses a plug. Never put a hand in the drain, even with the power off.

2

Free a humming jam

Insert the hex key that came with the unit into the center socket on the bottom and rock it back and forth until it turns freely. Then press the red reset button on the bottom of the disposal.

3

Contain a leak

Place a bucket under the unit, stop the dishwasher and move cleaning supplies out of the cabinet. If water is steady, close the sink's supply stops or the main house valve.

4

Note the brand and horsepower

The label on the side gives the model and the horsepower. Photograph it so we can bring a matching seal, gasket or replacement.

Why disposals jam, hum or go silent

A hum with no spin means the motor is getting power but the grinder plate is stuck. Common culprits are bones, fruit pits, a bottle cap or a spoon that slipped in. Once freed with a hex key, most units spin again, though a bent shredder ring can jam again soon.

Complete silence is different. That points to a tripped reset button, a dead wall switch, a loose wire in the junction box, or a burned-out motor. We test each of those with a meter before we call the unit bad.

Where a disposal leaks, and how each leak gets fixed

Top flange and sink seal

Plumber's putty or a gasket under the sink flange dries out and cracks. We take the unit down, clean the sink opening, and reseat it with a new seal and mounting ring.

Dishwasher inlet and drain pipe

The dishwasher hose clamp can loosen from vibration. The elbow and discharge pipe also have rubber gaskets that fail. These are low-cost repairs when caught early.

Bottom seam

Water from the bottom or from a seam in the body means the housing or the internal seal has failed. A repair rarely holds, so replacement is the sound choice.

What should stay out of the disposal

Most calls we get trace back to a short list of items.

  • Grease, fat and cooking oil, which coat the pipe walls and harden.
  • Bones, fruit pits and corn cobs.
  • Coffee grounds in large amounts.
  • Pasta, rice and potato peels, which swell into paste.
  • Fibrous scraps such as celery, onion skins and artichoke leaves.
  • Eggshells, if the line has slow drainage already.
  • Non-food items like bottle caps, utensils and glass.
  • Harsh chemical drain cleaners, which can damage seals and splash back.

Replacing a disposal: horsepower, mounting and hookup

Most homes need a 1/2 or 3/4 horsepower unit, and a 1 horsepower model suits heavy use. Continuous-feed units run while the switch is on, and batch-feed models run only with the stopper in place. Both need a three-prong cord or hardwired connection and a drain trap that fits.

One classic mistake is skipping the dishwasher knockout. If the new unit has a plug on the dishwasher inlet and the plug is not removed, the dishwasher cannot drain. We check that during every install and test the cycle. If your sink also drips at the faucet, our faucet repair service can cover it in the same visit.
